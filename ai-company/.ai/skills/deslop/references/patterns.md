# Pattern catalogue

Each entry: what it looks like, why it reads as generated, how to fix it. Fix means the smallest change that removes the pattern while keeping the writer's meaning and voice.

## 1. Openers and setups

**Throat-clearing.** "Here's the thing", "Here's what I mean", "Let me be clear", "I'll be honest", "The uncomfortable truth is", "It turns out", "Can we talk about". These promise a point instead of making it. *Fix:* delete the phrase; the next clause is usually the sentence.

**Faux-insight framing.** "What nobody tells you about X", "The part everyone misses", "Most people get this wrong". They cast the writer as the lone expert and delay the claim. *Fix:* delete the frame; state the claim; let its content earn attention.

**Rhetorical priming.** "What if I told you...", "Think about it:", "Imagine a world where...", "Ever wondered why...". *Fix:* answer the question you were about to ask, as a statement.

**Credential opener.** "As a [role], I've seen...". *Fix:* cut it. If the credential matters, it appears naturally in a specific example ("When I ran the on-call rota at Allegro...").

**Situational scene-setting with no scene.** "In today's fast-paced world", "In the age of AI", "In an ever-changing landscape". *Fix:* delete. If the context matters, name the actual change ("Since the EU AI Act came into force in August 2026...").

## 2. Contrast and reveal structures

**Binary contrast.** "It's not X. It's Y." / "The question isn't X, it's Y." / "This isn't about X, it's about Y." / "Not just X but Y." The negation is a fake setup; the reader only needed Y. *Fix:* write Y as a plain claim. "The question isn't the model, it's the eval" becomes "The eval matters more than the model."

**Negative runway.** "Not a tool. Not a framework. A way of thinking." Three negations as a drum roll. *Fix:* "It's a way of thinking", then say what that means in practice.

**Transformation arc.** "X stops being a cost and starts being an asset." *Fix:* name what changed and who changed it.

**Colon reveal.** Noun phrase, colon, dramatic lowercase payoff: "The detail that makes it work: a second agent grades it." *Fix:* plain sentence. "A second agent grades the output, which is what makes it work." Keep colons for lists, labels, and quotes.

**Self-answered question.** "Why does this matter? Because..." *Fix:* delete the question, keep the because-clause as a sentence.

## 3. Commentary about the text itself

**Importance labels.** "This is crucial", "This distinction matters", "That last part matters more than it sounds", "Importantly", "Notably", "Interestingly". *Fix:* delete. If the point is not visibly important without the label, add the fact that makes it so.

**Redundant glossing.** "In other words", "To put it simply", "As you can see", "In essence", followed by a restatement. *Fix:* keep whichever version is clearer, delete the other and the connector.

**Structural narration.** "In this section we'll...", "Let me walk you through...", "The rest of this post explains...", "But that's a topic for another day". *Fix:* delete. Headings and order do this job.

**Emphasis crutches.** "Full stop.", "Period.", "Let that sink in.", "Make no mistake", "This matters because". *Fix:* delete.

## 4. Agency and voice

**False agency.** Inanimate things performing human verbs: "the decision emerged", "the culture shifted", "the data tells us", "a complaint becomes a fix", "the market rewards". Generated prose does this to avoid naming who did what. *Fix:* find the person. "We decided in the March review", "the team changed how it triages", "I read the numbers and concluded".

**Passive without reason.** "Mistakes were made", "It was decided", "The feature is considered stable". *Fix:* name the actor. Keep passive only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or the object is the topic ("The bridge was built in 1932").

**Narrator from a distance.** "Nobody designed this", "People tend to", "Teams often struggle with". Floating above the scene. *Fix:* put a specific person, or the reader, in the room. "You don't sit down one day and decide to..."

**Corporate pep talk.** Cheerful, frictionless, everything is an opportunity. *Fix:* include the part that didn't work, the cost, or the trade-off the writer actually faced. Do not invent one.

**Fake-strong verbs.** "Serves as a centralized hub for", "acts as a catalyst for", "functions as". *Fix:* "is" or "has" plus the concrete function. "The app tracks sponsors, drafts, and approvals in one place."

## 5. Claims without support

**Weasel attribution.** "Experts agree", "Studies show", "Industry reports suggest", "It is widely believed", "Many argue". *Fix:* name the source and date, or delete the claim, or ask the user for the source. Never invent one.

**Vague declaratives.** "The implications are significant", "The stakes are high", "The reasons are structural", "This has far-reaching consequences". *Fix:* replace with the specific implication, stake, reason, or consequence. If the writer doesn't have one, delete the sentence.

**Importance puffery.** "Marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament to", "plays a vital role", "underscores its commitment to", "solidifies its position as". *Fix:* state what happened. "The launch marks a pivotal moment" becomes "The launch is the company's first paid product."

**Trailing -ing gloss.** A sentence that ends with "highlighting / underscoring / showcasing / reflecting / demonstrating [abstract virtue]". It pretends to analyse. *Fix:* delete the clause, or replace it with the concrete consequence. "...adds file search, highlighting the team's focus on productivity" becomes "...adds file search, so users can find old drafts without leaving the editor."

**Lazy extremes.** "Everyone", "always", "never", "nobody", "every single" used as emphasis rather than fact. *Fix:* the actual proportion or a specific case, or drop the quantifier.

## 6. Rhythm and shape

**Metronome.** Three or more consecutive sentences of similar length. Readers feel this before they can name it. *Fix:* merge two, or split one, following the meaning.

**Staccato drama.** "Speed. Quality. Cost. Pick two. That's it." Fragments stacked for profundity. *Fix:* one sentence. "You get two of speed, quality, and cost."

**Template paragraphs.** Every paragraph runs topic sentence, explanation, example, transition. *Fix:* let some paragraphs be one sentence, let some end without a bridge, let some start with the example.

**Rule of three.** Adjectives, examples, and list items arriving in threes by habit. *Fix:* use the number the content has. Two is fine. Five is fine.

**Parallel sections.** Every section the same length with the same internal structure. *Fix:* give each point the treatment it needs; unequal is normal.

**Synonym cycling.** "The agent reviews the draft. The assistant scores it. The tool suggests fixes." Rotating nouns for variety confuses the reader about how many things there are. *Fix:* repeat the clear word.

**Punctuation habits.** Em dashes as the default connector, exclamation marks for enthusiasm, ellipses as transitions. *Fix:* commas, periods, parentheses, semicolons where a real pause or link exists. Budget for an ordinary-length piece: an em dash or two, one exclamation mark at most, ellipsis only for a real trailing-off.

## 7. Endings

**Fake-profound kicker.** A final line that turns the point into a metaphor or aphorism: "The future isn't coming. It's already here." "Build the thing. The rest follows." *Fix:* delete it. Do not replace it with a better aphorism. End on the last concrete sentence already in the draft, or add a plain next step.

**Recap paragraph.** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", "To sum up", or an unlabelled final paragraph that restates the piece. *Fix:* delete. The reader was just there.

**Permission granting.** "And that's okay." "And that's fine." *Fix:* delete.

**Call-to-action boilerplate.** "I hope this helps", "Feel free to reach out", "Let me know if you have any questions" when nobody asked. *Fix:* delete, or replace with the one specific thing you want the reader to do.

## 8. Vocabulary habits

See `words.md` for lists. General rule: a word on the list inside a quote, a proper noun, code, or a deliberate joke stays. A word doing generic work goes, and the replacement is usually a more specific noun or verb, not a synonym.

## 9. Things that are not slop

Do not "fix" these; over-correction is its own tell.

- A short sentence after a long one, used once, for emphasis.
- A rhetorical question the writer actually goes on to explore rather than answer in the next clause.
- "I think", "probably", "I'm not sure" when the writer is genuinely unsure.
- An em dash where the aside really is an aside.
- A list, when the content is a list.
- A personal story that sets up tension or context, even if it delays the point.
- Repetition of a key term.
- A three-item list when there are three items.
- Passive voice when the actor is unknown or unimportant.
- Profanity, slang, or dry humour that belongs to the writer.
- Long sentences that are clear.
